# Spanish Lesson 1: sources and open questions

Date: 2026-11-06. Lesson file: `channel/episodes/spanish-1.js`. **A Spanish speaker should review this lesson before anyone relies on it.** Nobody who speaks Spanish has checked the script, and nobody has heard the voices.

## What we followed from Oak
We follow Oak National Academy's Year 7 Spanish program. Oak hasn't seen or approved this lesson. We say "follows", never "approved".

- Program: [Year 7 Spanish units](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/spanish-secondary-ks3/units?year=year-7). First unit of 14: Identity: 'estar' and 'ser', 6 lessons.
- Lesson: [Greetings in the Spanish-speaking world: 'estar' 1st and 3rd person singular](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/spanish-secondary-ks3/units/identity-estar-and-ser/lessons/greetings-in-the-spanish-speaking-world-estar-1st-and-3rd-person-singular)
- Objective: use estoy and está to say where you and others are, and recognise and pronounce the sound [a].
- Order, which we kept: (1) the sound [a], (2) estar for location, (3) greetings.
- Key points we teach: [a] has one sound; estar means to be and is used for location; estoy and está are its first and third person singular; hola means hello and hasta luego means bye.
- Oak's wrong idea, which opens our lesson: thinking [a] sounds the same in Spanish and English. In English it varies, and in Spanish it doesn't.
- Teaching moves we kept, in our own words: listen for which words have the sound, compare animal, plant and May with their Spanish forms, tell apart estoy and está by ear, fill the gap in a sentence, and interpret English speech aloud.
- Starter questions follow Oak's prior knowledge check on verbs and on "I" against "she" and "it".
- Oak's license: Open Government Licence v3.0, "except where otherwise stated". We used no Oak text, slides, audio or video, so no Oak credit is needed on screen. We only follow the structure.

## Where we differ from Oak, and why
| Oak | Our lesson | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Places in Spain: Alhambra, Guggenheim, Bernabéu | Chichén Itzá, Machu Picchu, Bogotá, Cartagena, Madrid, London | The program covers Colombia, and the owner asked for Latin American Spanish |
| Leeds está en Inglaterra | Londres está en Inglaterra | Our own example. It also shows that place names can change |
| gafas (glasses) in the read-aloud list | Left out | The word for glasses changes by country, so it isn't neutral. Unverified from memory: lentes is common in Mexico |
| estás in the vocabulary list | Only heard in the teaser | Oak's objective for this lesson is estoy and está. Estás is Lesson 2 |
| Adds nothing on ñ | Short note on ñ | España is on Oak's list, and children can't say it without ñ. The brief also asked for it |
| Written accents aren't a key point | Short note on the line over á | Children see it in está and Panamá and may think it changes the sound |

**Spain and Latin America.** Nothing in the lesson's taught Spanish differs between them: estoy, está, estar, en, dónde, hola and hasta luego are universal. Vosotros never appears. Chao is mentioned as common in many Latin American countries, following Oak's own note. The bonus shows papa (Latin America) against patata (Spain), and it isn't tested.
