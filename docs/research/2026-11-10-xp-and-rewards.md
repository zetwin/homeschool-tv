# XP and rewards: what real products do, and what the evidence says (2026-11-10)

Research for `product/XP-AND-REWARDS.md`. The children asked for XP (points) for everything they do, and a shop where they spend it on real rewards. The owner's words are in the team's private notes (2026-11-10).

**How solid this is.** Most product facts come from help pages, app-store pages and reviews, which change often. One study was read in full: Deci, Koestner and Ryan (2001). The rest come from abstracts and summaries. Anything not checked on a real page is marked **UNVERIFIED**, and listed again at the end.

Words used below:
- **Intrinsic interest:** doing something because you like it.
- **Expected reward:** a reward promised before the task ("do this and you get that").
- **Token economy:** points or tokens that are swapped later for real rewards. This is exactly what the children asked for.

**Pattern across all of them:** parents decide the rewards and prices; children claim; points are deducted; household jobs need a parent's OK. Only Duolingo and Prodigy rank children or sell things, and those are the parts with the most criticism.

### Token economies in schools
- **A 2022 review of 24 studies** in primary classrooms (ages about 5 to 11) found **large effects** on behaviour, in both general and special education. [Kim and others 2022, Behavior Modification](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-token-economy-practices-in/) (abstract checked).
- **An earlier review (Maggin and others, 2011)** said the evidence was **not yet enough to call token economies "best practice"**, though well-run ones can work. [Catalogue record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c406t-80e6c2060db26a54b46f400b5a8594d3cb59d23a6391aa3a236b03b83c9396e33) (abstract only, **UNVERIFIED** in detail).
- These studies measure **behaviour** (doing the work, calm class), mostly in the short term. They don't show long-term love of learning.

## 2. What the evidence says

### Rewards can lower interest: the overjustification effect
- **The classic study (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett, 1973).** Preschool children who liked drawing were split into three groups: promised a certificate, given one as a surprise, or given nothing. Later, only the **promised** group drew less in free time. [Paper PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/undermining-children-s-intrinsic-interest-with-extrinsic-3qn44yqiou.pdf) (summary seen; full text not read).
- **The big review (Deci, Koestner and Ryan, 128 experiments).** Read in full in its 2001 education version: [Review of Educational Research PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf); original: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/). What it found ("d" is the size of the effect; minus means less interest later):

| Kind of reward | Effect on doing the task freely later |
|---|---|
| Praise and positive feedback | **Up** (d = +0.33) for adults, but **no clear effect for children** (d = +0.11). Praise given in a controlling way ("good, you did what you should") **lowered** interest (d = -0.44); informative praise raised it (d = +0.66) |
| Tangible rewards (prizes, money, certificates) overall | **Down** (d = -0.34), and **worse for children than for students** |
| **Unexpected** tangible rewards | **No harm** (d = +0.01) |
| Rewards for just taking part | Down (d = -0.40) |
| Rewards for finishing | Down (d = -0.36) |
| Rewards for doing well | Down (d = -0.28). The authors warn this underestimates real life, where many children *don't* get the reward |
| Rewards for **dull** tasks | **No harm** (d = +0.18, not significant): there was little interest to lose. But rewards don't make a dull task interesting either |

  - The harm was strongest when measured **a week or more later**.
  - Their advice to teachers: focus on more interesting tasks, **more choice**, and the right level of challenge, rather than rewards.
- **The other side (Cameron and Pierce, 1994; 96 experiments)** concluded that rewards do **not** lower interest overall, that praise raises it, and that harm happens only in narrow conditions that are "easily avoided". [Review of Educational Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543064003363). The debate is still open; both sides agree that **unexpected rewards and praise don't harm**, and that **expected tangible rewards are the risky kind**.

**For us:** chores are "dull tasks", so points for chores are low-risk. Lessons, activities, reading and chess are things a child may already enjoy, so big promised prizes for them carry the most risk. That shapes the plan.

### When rewards help
- **Reward effort and actions, not results.** In randomised trials across US cities, paying pupils for **inputs** (for example, $2 per book read and checked by a quiz, in Dallas) raised reading results; paying for **outputs** (test scores) didn't. Pupils didn't know how to turn "get a higher score" into actions. [NBER paper w15898](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15898) (number from search, page not opened), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2011/02/23/133632394/should-we-pay-kids-to-study), [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/does-paying-kids-to-do-well-in-school-actually-work).
  - Caveat in the same work: in the Dallas study, English learners who preferred Spanish read worse by the end. The reason is unclear.
- **Praise the process, not the child's cleverness.** Ten-to-eleven-year-olds praised for being clever chose easier tasks and did worse after failure than those praised for effort. [Mueller and Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf).
- **The three needs (self-determination theory).** Children stay motivated when they feel **choice** (autonomy), **able** (competence) and **close to others** (relatedness). Rewards that feel controlling hurt the first; rewards that say "you got better" help the second. [APA overview](https://www.apa.org/research-practice/conduct-research/self-determination-theory); Ryan and Deci 2020, Contemporary Educational Psychology, [DOI 10.1016/j.cedpsych.2020.101860](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2020.101860) (link not opened; **UNVERIFIED**).

### Badges, leaderboards and competition
- **A 16-week university course** with badges, a leaderboard and coins ended with **lower motivation, lower satisfaction and lower exam scores** than the same course without them. One study, adults, and it can't separate the leaderboard from the badges. [Hanus and Fox 2015, in a critical review](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13).
- Already in our earlier research: badges and a leaderboard in one course lowered results ([UCLA](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)); a meta-analysis reportedly favours no leaderboards (**UNVERIFIED**). See `research/2026-11-10-activities-learning-science.md`.

### Streaks
- **Broken streaks make people stop.** Once a streak breaks, people do the activity less afterwards; the effect is weaker when they can "repair" the streak. [Silverman and Barasch 2023, Journal of Consumer Research, summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions) (abstract-level only).
- **Some slack helps.** Duolingo cites a Penn and UCLA study that flexibility is more motivating than rigid rules. [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit).
- **"Streak anxiety"** as lasting harm is mostly practitioner opinion, not well studied (**UNVERIFIED**). Example of the opinion: [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/streak-design-gamification-motivation-burnout/).

### Gaming the system
- **Children find the shortest path to points.** Researchers call it "gaming the system": fast guessing, clicking through hints. It's linked to weaker learning, and it happens more when tasks are confusing or abstract. [Baker, retrospective](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/PSCS-gaming-v6.pdf).
- Duolingo's XP farming (above) is the same thing at scale.

### Fairness between siblings
- **Fair-seeming matters more than equal.** Children accept being treated differently from a sibling when they understand the reason and think it's fair; unequal treatment that feels **unfair** goes with worse relationships and more problems. [Kowal, Kramer, Krull and Crick 2002](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northeastern.edu/dist/d/3128/files/2023/05/Kowal-Kramer-Krull-Crick-JFP-2002.pdf), [later review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5110249).
- **For us:** the same rules for every child, written where all three can read them, and XP for doing (which every age can do), not for scores (where the oldest wins). No research found on sibling points systems (**UNVERIFIED**, our judgement).

### Screen time and sweets as rewards
- **Screen time as a reward:** a small Canadian survey (parents of children aged 18 months to 5 years) found that children whose screens were used as a reward or punishment had about 20 minutes more screen time a day. A link, not a cause, and much younger children than ours. [University of Guelph, via Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/controlling-children-s-behavior-with-screen-time-leads-to-more-screen-time-study-reveals).
- **Sweets as a reward:** adults who remembered sweets being used to control them as children reported more binge eating ([Puhl and Schwartz 2003, Times Higher Education report](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/43001)). That relies on memory. A later study following children over time found **no clear link** from food rewards to later eating, and said firm evidence is lacking ([PMC, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7583369), **UNVERIFIED** that the authors are as named in summaries).
- **For us:** both are allowed, because parents decide; but the starting shop leads with non-food, non-screen rewards, and parents can set a weekly limit.

## 3. What this means for the design (our judgement)
1. **Points for doing and fixing, never for marks.** Matches Khan's effort points, Fryer's inputs, and the risk of performance rewards. It's also fairer across ages.
2. **XP is never taken away as punishment.** Habitica's penalties and Duolingo's lost streaks are the parts with the clearest harm.
3. **Keep promised rewards small for things children already enjoy;** bigger for dull jobs (chores), where rewards do no harm.
4. **Surprises and praise from parents** are the safest rewards of all. Make them easy.
5. **A family goal** beats ranking. Leaderboards have the weakest record.
6. **Forgiving streaks with automatic rest days,** never sold.
7. **Rules that can't be farmed:** XP once per thing, no XP for re-watching or rushing.
8. **Children help set the shop** (choice), and see the rules (fairness).
9. **Watch for warning signs** after a few weeks: "how many XP is it?" before every task, refusing un-pointed work, rushing. Then shift weight from XP to surprises and the family goal. Token-economy practice normally plans to fade points over time (**UNVERIFIED** for our age group; a common teacher-training rule, not checked at a source).
