# Rules for titles, names and images

Last updated: 2026-11-06. **Read this before writing or changing any lesson.** The owner has had to correct these mistakes more than once: a whale on a human skeleton lesson, a climber as a lesson cover, and intestines on a Muscles cover.

## The main test
Show the title and the picture to someone who knows nothing about the lesson, for 3 seconds. They must be able to say what the lesson is about. If they can't, or they name another topic, it's wrong, however clever it is.

## Titles
1. **The lesson title says its topic in plain words.** It should contain the topic word: "How Do Your Muscles Work?"
2. **A question is good, a riddle isn't.** The surprise belongs inside the lesson, not in its name. "Who Moves Your Breakfast?" made a Muscles lesson sound like a lesson about food.
3. **Never name a lesson after a side detail,** a joke, or one fun fact from it.
4. **A chapter title says what that chapter teaches.**
5. Lesson 1, "Why Don't You Collapse?", is the one exception so far. Its topic word is missing, but a skeleton is on the cover and the owner liked it. Don't copy it without the same test.

## Names
1. **Lists, the program and reports use the official lesson name:** "Muscles", "The human skeleton".
2. **The same thing keeps the same name everywhere.** Use "lesson" and "program". Don't invent a new word for something that already has one.
3. **Use words a 10-year-old knows,** and explain a new science word when it first appears.

## Images
1. **The cover shows the lesson's topic, and only that.** Muscles lesson: muscles. No second topic on the cover, drawn or photographed.
2. **Don't use another topic to be interesting.** Another body system, an animal or a sport appears only when the sentence being spoken is about it, and only in that scene.
3. **Each chapter picture matches that chapter's title,** not just the lesson's general topic.
4. **"It's related" isn't enough.** The check is "does this show what's being said right now?"
5. **After any zoom or crop, check what's still visible,** and that no words are cut off.
6. **No impressive picture for its own sake.**
7. **Never publish a picture nobody has looked at.** An automatic search produced sailors for "Forces" and an absinthe spoon for "Solutions". The owner saw them live. A person or Claude must view every picture against its topic first.
9. **Program and category blocks use professional icons,** from Microsoft's Fluent Emoji 3D set (MIT license), chosen in `channel/unit-art.js`. **Claude doesn't hand-draw icons or illustrations.** The owner rejected them as poor. Key art should come from a professional set, a licensed library, or an illustrator.
8. **Education first:** a picture should look like it belongs in a good school book. If no good one exists, use a clean subject tile.

## Content
1. **The official objectives lead.** A fun fact supports the topic. It never takes over the opening, the title or the cover.
2. **When something from another topic is needed, tie it to this one out loud.** Oak's Muscles lesson includes smooth muscle moving food, so the scene talks about the *muscle* doing the squeezing, not about digestion.

## Variety within one style
Lessons keep one feeling: the same colors, fonts, voice and show structure. What changes:
- **Each chapter opening has a different layout and entrance.** Two chapters in a row must never look the same.
- **Only chapter changes get the big transition.** Scenes inside a chapter change quietly.
- **Add a new idea only when it clearly suits that moment** (a second voice, a home experiment, a live question). Variety must never become a mess.

## The check before showing the owner
1. Check every title and picture against this page.
2. Ask a **fresh agent with no background** to do the main test, with first-look instructions (kept with the team's private working files). It sees only the screens, and gets none of my reasoning.
3. Fix what it finds, and only then show the owner.
